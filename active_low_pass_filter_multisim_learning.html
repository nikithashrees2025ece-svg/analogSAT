1. Introduction and learning objectives

An active low-pass filter passes low-frequency components more strongly and attenuates high-frequency components. It combines a resistor-capacitor network with an operational amplifier, allowing buffering or gain while shaping the frequency response.

After studying this page, you should be able to

  • Identify the inverting active low-pass topology in your Multisim schematic.
  • Derive its transfer function, cutoff frequency, gain and phase response.
  • Interpret the actual Bode-plot cursor reading without confusing it with the cutoff frequency.
  • Spot practical simulation issues such as excessive transient input amplitude and op-amp supply wiring.

Prerequisites and terminology

Reactance: opposition offered by a capacitor to AC, \(X_C=1/(2\pi fC)\). Cutoff frequency: frequency where the magnitude is 0.707 of the passband magnitude (−3.01 dB relative). Bode plot: gain and/or phase versus logarithmic frequency. Passband: frequency range that is passed with relatively little attenuation.

3. Theory and derivation

Step 1 — Feedback impedance

R1 and C1 are in parallel. The capacitor impedance is \(Z_C=1/(sC)\), so:

Zf = R1 || 1/(sC1) = R1 / (1 + sR1C1)

Step 2 — Transfer function

For an ideal inverting op-amp with negative feedback, \(V_-/\approx V_+=0\) (virtual ground) and input current approximately equals feedback current. Thus \(H(s)=Vout/Vin=-Zf/R2\):

H(s) = −(R1/R2) / (1 + sR1C1)

This is a first-order low-pass response. The minus sign means the output is inverted at low frequencies.

Step 3 — Cutoff frequency

fc = 1 / (2π R1 C1)
fc = 1 / (2π × 1000 Ω × 1 × 10⁻⁶ F) = 159.15 Hz (approximately)

At \(f= f_c\), the magnitude is \(1/\sqrt{2}\) of the low-frequency gain, which is −3.01 dB relative to passband.

Magnitude, decibels and phase

|H(jω)| = (R1/R2) / √(1 + (f/fc)²)
Gain (dB) = 20 log₁₀ |H(jω)|
Phase = −180° − tan⁻¹(f/fc) (equivalent wrapped phase may display as +180° − tan⁻¹(f/fc))

For R1 = R2 = 1 kΩ, the passband magnitude is 1 V/V = 0 dB. The phase is near ±180° at low frequency and approaches the equivalent of −270° (or +90° wrapped) at high frequency.

DC and AC behaviour

  • DC / very low frequency: C1 behaves approximately open-circuit, so gain tends to −R1/R2 = −1.
  • At cutoff: gain magnitude is 0.707 of passband, or −3.01 dB relative to passband.
  • High frequency: C1 increasingly bypasses R1, feedback impedance falls, and gain magnitude tends toward zero.

4. Simulation output and interpretation

What the screenshot supports: at the selected cursor point, Multisim displays 125.574 Hz and −2.103 dB. That is the measured gain at the cursor, not the cutoff frequency itself.

Compare theory with your cursor

For the ideal values R1 = R2 = 1 kΩ and C1 = 1 µF, the theoretical gain magnitude at 125.574 Hz is:

|H| = 1 / √(1 + (125.574/159.15)²) ≈ 0.785 V/V
Gain ≈ 20 log₁₀(0.785) ≈ −2.10 dB

Theoretical result is approximately −2.10 dB, which closely matches the screenshot reading of −2.103 dB. This agreement is consistent with the intended first-order response, within cursor and model precision.

Recommended simulation procedure

  1. Keep the Bode plotter input connected from the circuit input to ground, and output measurement from filter output to ground, with the positive/negative terminals correctly oriented.
  2. Run the frequency sweep over a range that includes values well below and above 159 Hz, such as 1 Hz to 100 kHz.
  3. Place the cursor at about 159.15 Hz. For this circuit, the gain should be approximately −3.01 dB relative to the 0 dB passband.
  4. For transient oscilloscope tests, use a much smaller input amplitude compatible with the supply rails, e.g. around 0.1–1 V peak, rather than 120 Vrms.
  5. Record the source amplitude, frequency, supply rails and actual output measurement with each screenshot.
Potential transient issue: 120 Vrms corresponds to about 170 V peak for a sine wave, far beyond ±12 V rails. A real or realistically modelled op-amp cannot reproduce that without clipping/saturation. The Bode plot is a small-signal frequency-response measurement; do not interpret it as proof that a 120 Vrms time-domain input will be amplified linearly.

5. Solved numerical examples

Example 1 — Cutoff frequency (easy)

Given: R1 = 1 kΩ, C1 = 1 µF. Find fc.

fc = 1/(2πR1C1) = 1/(2π × 1000 × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 159.15 Hz

Answer: 159.15 Hz. The filter begins its first-order roll-off around this frequency.

Example 2 — Gain at 125.574 Hz (moderate)

Given: R1 = R2 = 1 kΩ, fc = 159.15 Hz, f = 125.574 Hz.

|H| = 1/√(1 + (125.574/159.15)²) ≈ 0.785
GdB = 20log₁₀(0.785) ≈ −2.10 dB

Answer: approximately −2.10 dB, matching the cursor value shown in your screenshot.

Example 3 — Gain at 1 kHz (GATE-style)

Given: R1 = R2, fc = 159.15 Hz, f = 1000 Hz.

|H| = 1/√(1 + (1000/159.15)²) ≈ 0.157
GdB ≈ 20log₁₀(0.157) ≈ −16.1 dB

Answer: approximately 0.157 V/V or −16.1 dB. Above cutoff, the first-order filter attenuates at approximately 20 dB/decade.

7. Core electronics interview questions

1. What is a low-pass filter?

A circuit whose gain is higher at lower frequencies and decreases above a characteristic cutoff frequency.

2. Why is this circuit called an inverting filter?

The input signal is applied through R2 to the inverting input, while the non-inverting input is grounded. Its low-frequency transfer gain is negative.

3. Why does the capacitor go in the feedback path?

Its impedance decreases with frequency, reducing the feedback impedance and therefore the closed-loop gain at high frequencies.

4. What is the cutoff frequency for this circuit?

Ideally, fc = 1/(2πR1C1), approximately 159.15 Hz for 1 kΩ and 1 µF.

5. What does −3 dB mean at cutoff?

The magnitude is about 0.707 of its passband value; the power ratio is one half for the same impedance.

6. How would you validate the filter in a lab?

Run an AC sweep, measure input/output magnitude at each frequency, calculate 20log10(Vout/Vin), and find the frequency 3.01 dB below passband.

7. Why might the output clip in transient simulation?

The signal may exceed the op-amp's output swing or common-mode range. The 120 Vrms source shown is far too large for ±12 V rails.

8. What is the expected roll-off?

Approximately −20 dB per decade above cutoff for a first-order pole.

9. What non-ideal op-amp properties affect the result?

Finite gain-bandwidth product, slew rate, input offset/bias, output swing, loading and supply limits can change the response.

10. What is a common Bode-plot wiring error?

Incorrect IN/OUT polarity or measuring against the wrong ground/reference can produce a misleading gain or phase trace. Verify all four Bode plotter terminals.

11. How can the cutoff be changed?

Increase R1 or C1 to lower fc; decrease either to raise fc, since fc is inversely proportional to their product.

12. Where is this circuit used?

Sensor conditioning, noise reduction, audio conditioning, and limiting high-frequency noise before an ADC, subject to suitable component and op-amp selection.

8. Applications, advantages and limitations

Applications

  • Sensor and instrumentation signal conditioning
  • Audio-band filtering and noise reduction
  • Anti-alias filtering before ADC sampling (with a suitable design order and cutoff)
  • Analog front ends and measurement systems

Advantages

  • Simple design with a predictable first-order response
  • Active gain and filtering in one stage
  • Easy to simulate and test using AC sweep

Limitations

  • First-order attenuation is only about 20 dB/decade
  • Real op-amp bandwidth and output swing are finite
  • Component tolerances shift the actual cutoff

Common errors to avoid

  • Do not call the cursor frequency the cutoff frequency unless the cursor is at the −3 dB point relative to the passband.
  • Do not apply a 120 Vrms signal to a ±12 V op-amp in a transient test and expect linear output.
  • Check that the non-inverting input is grounded as shown and that R1 and C1 are both connected in parallel in the feedback path.
  • Check the actual op-amp power pin polarity, not only the displayed “12 V” labels.
  • Distinguish Bode small-signal AC analysis from time-domain transient analysis.
